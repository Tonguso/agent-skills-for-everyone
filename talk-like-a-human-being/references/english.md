# English Editing Guide

Use this guide to check the agent's own English replies before sending. Follow the user's language and tone preferences and higher-priority instructions. References below to a source, author, or edit mean the agent's draft and its evidence unless the user explicitly requests editing supplied text. These are contextual wording checks, not AI-authorship signals or a mandate to rewrite the user's writing.

## How to use a signal

For each possible issue, identify the actual wording and its function. Then choose the smallest edit that fixes a real problem. A signal can be normal, effective, or required by the genre. Do not edit because a word appears in an example list, because several weak signals co-occur, or because you want the prose to look less polished.

### 1. Inflated importance and notability

**Look for:** Abstract claims that an event, tool, or idea is “a pivotal moment,” “a testament to,” “a transformative force,” “a groundbreaking milestone,” or “a cornerstone” without saying what changed or why it matters. Check whether a string of grand claims repeats the same importance claim.

**Edit when:** The wording adds scale or praise but no distinct information. Prefer the concrete action, result, or limitation already present.

**Keep when:** The claim is a necessary opinion, a quotation, a defined term, or supported by the surrounding evidence. Do not delete a claim merely because it is abstract or emphatic.

### 2. Vague attribution and borrowed authority

**Look for:** “Experts say,” “industry observers believe,” “research shows,” “widely regarded,” or “the media has called it” when the passage gives no identifiable source.

**Edit carefully:** Do not strip the attribution and leave its claim sounding like the author's own verified fact. Preserve the attribution and flag that it is unspecified, or ask for a source if the task calls for fact-checking. Never invent a person, publication, date, or citation.

**Keep when:** The phrase accurately describes a real, intentionally general consensus or appears as quoted material under discussion.

### 3. Promotional copy and generic prospects

**Look for:** Clusters of superlatives, unsupported benefits, “unlock potential,” “seamless experience,” “new era,” or a closing that promises a bright future after a generic mention of challenges.

**Edit when:** It pads an informational passage or makes evidence sound stronger than it is. Replace only with a factual description already supported by the source; otherwise retain the claim or flag it rather than fabricating proof.

**Keep when:** The task is explicitly marketing, advocacy, a quotation, or a genre where aspirational language is part of the brief. Even then, preserve the user's intended degree of persuasion.

### 4. Filler, throat-clearing, and false insight

**Look for:** Openers such as “It is important to note that,” “In today's rapidly changing world,” “Let's dive in,” “When it comes to,” or “The answer is simple:” that delay a point; and a second sentence that restates the first as a supposed nuance (“More precisely,” “That said,” “In reality”).

**Edit when:** Removing the phrase leaves the same point, relationship, and tone. Start with the subject or claim; retain a transition if it genuinely connects ideas, and retain a correction if it adds evidence or changes the conclusion.

**Keep when:** The framing serves a real audience need, signals an important caveat, or is part of the writer's voice.

### 5. Chatbot residue and flattery

**Look for:** “Great question!”, “I hope this helps,” “Feel free to ask,” or model limitations such as “as an AI” in an article, report, or other text that is not a live exchange about an AI system.

**Edit when:** It is leftover assistant chatter rather than the content the author intends to publish. Remove only the chatter; preserve a real offer, service commitment, limitation, disclosure, or next step.

**Keep when:** Editing a real chat transcript, interface copy, or a text specifically about AI disclosure or system limitations.

### 6. Staged contrasts and negative parallelism

**Look for:** Repeated “not X, but Y,” “not only X, but also Y,” “it is not about X; it is about Y,” or “you may think X, but actually Y.” Ask whether the text has established the supposed misconception and whether the contrast contributes a real distinction.

**Edit when:** The first half invents an opponent or misconception just to dramatize an ordinary claim, or the same contrast frame recurs mechanically. State the supported claim directly while preserving any real difference between X and Y.

**Keep when:** The contrast corrects a genuine misunderstanding, makes a substantive distinction, or reflects real reasoning. Do not turn every positive/negative contrast into a flat assertion.

### 7. Lists, triads, and examples

**Look for:** Long comma-separated inventories, multiple synonym-like items, or several consecutive examples that all prove the same point.

**Edit when:** Items are genuinely redundant or the sentence is hard to follow. Group only items that mean the same thing; otherwise change the sentence shape and retain every distinct item. Keep necessary inventories intact.

**Keep when:** The list is complete by design, the items are distinct, the rhythm is intentional, or the format is a checklist, procedure, comparison, legal provision, or data report. Three items, parallel wording, or a question is not by itself a defect.

### 8. Repeated sentence and paragraph templates

**Look for:** Several adjacent sentences with the same grammatical frame, or multiple paragraphs that each make a claim, give one example, and end with the same significance sentence.

**Edit when:** The repeated frame makes distinct ideas sound mechanically generated. Recast one or two sentences around their actual relationship; preserve sequence, evidence, and paragraph logic.

**Keep when:** Repetition supports instructions, comparison, formal reporting, emphasis, or deliberate rhetoric. Do not measure sentence lengths or split paragraphs to create artificial “burstiness.” Similar lengths alone are not an edit signal.

### 9. Passive voice, nominalization, and sentence load

**Look for:** Several phrases such as “an analysis was conducted,” “consideration was given,” or “the implementation of the proposal” that hide the actor or bury the action; and clauses nested so deeply that the main point is hard to find.

**Edit when:** A direct verb makes the existing actor and action clearer. Keep the same agent, scope, and technical distinction. Split a sentence only when it improves comprehension without changing the logical relation.

**Keep when:** The actor is unknown or irrelevant; the passive is standard in a methods section, legal document, or technical description; the noun is a precise term; or the long sentence is readable and controlled. Passive voice, nominalization, and length are not errors by themselves.

### 10. Synonym cycling and vague intensifiers

**Look for:** Replacing a technical noun with a succession of near-synonyms to avoid repetition; “significantly,” “dramatically,” “substantially,” “more,” or “increasingly” with no clear measure or comparison target.

**Edit when:** Stable terminology would be clearer, or the comparison target is already explicit elsewhere in the text. Use the exact term consistently; do not add a measure.

**Keep when:** The terms really have different meanings, the comparison is understood from context, or the original deliberately qualifies an estimate. Do not turn an unquantified claim into a quantified one.

### 11. Vocabulary watch, not a blacklist

Words such as “delve,” “landscape,” “tapestry,” “pivotal,” “testament,” “underscore,” “multifaceted,” “robust,” and “foster,” or frames such as “in today's rapidly changing world,” can sound generic when several appear together or when they replace a specific point. That is a reason to inspect the sentence, not to search-and-replace the word. Keep a term that is precise, conventional in the field, part of a quotation, or characteristic of the author's voice.

### 12. Abstract agency and decorative metaphor

**Look for:** An abstract system “revolutionizes,” “unlocks,” “paves the way,” or “acts as a catalyst” without a concrete action; or an idealized tool-as-person metaphor that repeats an explanation without adding one.

**Edit when:** The metaphor merely inflates or repeats an already stated function. State what the source says the tool or organization does. If no action is supplied, keep the abstract claim or flag it; do not invent a mechanism.

**Keep when:** The image explains a real relationship, is distinctive to the writer, is quoted, or belongs to literary writing. Metaphor and figurative language are normal human choices.

### 13. Punctuation and formatting

**Look for:** Repeated em dashes used to create a reveal, cue-phrase colons (“The key is:”), empty sentences that announce a list, or bold inline labels and emojis used to make ordinary prose look like a template.

**Edit when:** The punctuation or formatting repeatedly substitutes for the sentence's logic or conflicts with the requested format. State the relationship directly and preserve all content.

**Keep when:** The punctuation is the author's habit, clarifies interruption or parenthesis, introduces a real quotation/list, or fits the medium. One em dash, colon, bold phrase, emoji, or numbered heading proves nothing.

### 14. Clickbait and engagement framing

**Look for:** “You won't believe,” shock numbers with no source, manufactured urgency, mystery endings, stacked credentials, or a promise to transform the reader's future.

**Edit when:** It is unintended residue in an explanatory text, or it makes a factual claim unsupported by the source. Preserve legitimate hooks and calls to action when the user is writing promotional or social copy.

## English patterns that are not automatic edits

Do not change a passage solely because it has uniform sentence length, a passive, an adverb, an em dash, a colon, a three-part list, a rhetorical question, a metaphor, first-person or third-person narration, a formal transition, a repeated technical noun, a short sentence, or a sentence fragment. Read its job in context. Do not force contractions, informal words, personal anecdotes, sentence fragments, or irregular grammar.

## English examples

**Inflated framing with a source detail already present**

Before: “The launch marks a transformative milestone for the team. The service now processes 4,000 requests a day, up from 2,500.”

Possible edit: “The launch is a major milestone for the team: the service now processes 4,000 requests a day, up from 2,500.”

Why: This keeps the author's judgment and the measurable change while trimming the inflated adjective. If “transformative” is a deliberate, supported judgment, keep it.

**Vague attribution**

Before: “Experts say the change will reshape the industry.”

Safe edit: No rewrite is justified from this text alone. Keep the source attribution and flag it as unspecified if the user asked for review.

Review note: A stylistic edit cannot supply the missing source. Do not add “some,” remove the attribution, or rewrite it as an established fact.

**A real contrast**

Before: “It isn't faster, but it is easier to maintain.”

Keep: This is a meaningful distinction, not empty contrast.

**Mechanical repetition**

Before: “The team reviewed the logs. The team found a timeout. The team opened an incident.”

Possible edit: “The team reviewed the logs, found a timeout, and opened an incident.”

Why: The same actor and sequence are clear; retain the order and all actions. Keep the separate sentences if the pacing is intentional.

## English final review

- Does every revised content claim trace back to the source?
- Are attribution, uncertainty, scope, time, causation, comparison, and responsibility intact?
- Did the edit solve a specific problem, or only make the text sound more like a generic editor?
- Did I preserve a real contrast, useful repetition, list, metaphor, formal register, or deliberate rhythm?
- Did I add slang, first person, facts, evidence, a joke, or a personal reaction that the source did not provide?
- Is anything outside the requested edit boundary changed?
